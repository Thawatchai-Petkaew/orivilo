## Evidence

Ben Mitchell (The Fontpad), on looped then loopless:

> "The first would commonly be used for body text and the second for titles and branding, and less commonly for body text;"
> "similar letters are distinguished by the orientation of loops."
> "Unlooped letters were normal in the Sukhothai period."

Proxima Nova Thai (Mark Simonson Studio, Thai by Cadson Demak):

> looped: "a more traditional style of the Thai script generally used for small text settings"
> loopless: "a more contemporary style used for display typography"

Smich Smanloh's ATypI 2020 abstract:

> "Thai-Latin type pairing, Thai Looped, and Loopless can all be paired with Serif and Sans in Latin"
> "Thai Looped and Loopless can be used interchangeably with both dans and Serif."

("dans" is the source's typo for sans.) A search summary of the Proxima Nova Thai story says balancing the bolder weights was one of the biggest hurdles, and stems were thickened so heavy weights sit with the Latin; that line was not confirmed on the fetched page.

Confidence 0.85: two independent type sources (a British type designer and a Thai foundry's product page) agree on looped for text and loopless for display; a third from the Thai foundry states pairing is flexible.

### What the sources do not show

- A size threshold for switching from looped to loopless; `brand-ci-method.md` §6 uses about 24 px, which no source here gives.
- Line-height or size-adjustment numbers for mixed Thai and Latin; none of the pages read gives them.
- Reader-preference research by age; the method's note that looped suits older readers was not found in these sources.

## Why it matters for ORIVILO

Thai and Latin typography is a CI element with its own rules, and the sources support the method's looped-for-reading, loopless-for-display split. The numbers in the method (24 px switch, 1.6 to 1.7 line-height) are not confirmed here and remain hypotheses.
