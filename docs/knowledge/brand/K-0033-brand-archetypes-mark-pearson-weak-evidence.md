## Evidence

Open Library record: title "The hero and the outlaw", subtitle "building extraordinary brands through the power of archetypes", by "Margaret Mark" and "Carol S. Pearson", "McGraw-Hill", "2001".

The publisher's description (via search, not read at the publisher) claims the most successful brands correspond to archetypes, using Nike, Marlboro and Ivory as cases. That is the authors' claim, not independent evidence.

The twelve archetype names as commonly listed (from secondary summaries, not the book): Innocent, Explorer, Sage, Hero, Outlaw, Magician, Regular Guy or Gal, Lover, Jester, Caregiver, Creator, Ruler. A search snippet of the book's contents shows "Part III: Leaving a Thumbprint on the World: Hero, Outlaw, Magician."

From the 2018 study's abstract:

> "checking if the archetypal framework of Mark and Pearson (2001) applies to different brands across countries."
> "received 537 valid questionnaires from Portugal, Brazil, Colombia and Peru,"
> "This consistency of results is significantly higher for word associations."
> "the perceptions of consumers expressed through the selected words"
> "were often diverse and heterogeneous among countries."

Confidence 0.7 for what is stated: the book exists with this thesis, and the evidence base is thin. The claim that archetypes make brands more successful is the authors' own and stays below 0.7; no source read tests it.

### What the sources do not show

- A reliability test of assigning a brand to an archetype (whether two strategists pick the same one).
- Any study linking archetype choice to business results.
- Any study with Thai consumers.

## Why it matters for ORIVILO

Archetypes are a common, easy-to-explain way to state brand personality, and clients may arrive with one. A tool may offer them as a vocabulary, but this entry cannot ground a rule that maps an archetype to a visual choice.
