## Evidence

From Romaniuk's article republished by the Institute:

> "a proper understanding of the existing strengths of a brand's distinctive assets, in terms of both uniqueness and fame."
> "shapes, colours, faces, words, fonts, moments."
> "part of a larger palette or menu of branding options."
> "The key thing is to remember the simple principle of contrast."
> "choose and execute for contrast against the visual and audio environment in which it is placed."

Search results for the book show chapters on measuring asset strength, fame, uniqueness and "The Grid", and a chapter on colour as an asset. A commentary summarises the grid's axes as prevalence ("how many people know the link") and uniqueness ("how many brands are linked to the element"), and argues uniqueness cannot be controlled by marketers because competitors affect it. The quadrant names seen in search ("Use or lose", "Avoid solo use", "Investment potential", "Ignore or test") come from a third-party summary and were not confirmed at a primary source.

Confidence 0.75: the fame and uniqueness pair and the contrast principle are quoted from the author; the grid's quadrants and the colour chapter were not read.

### What the sources do not show

- Colour-specific findings (for example how often colour alone triggers the brand) and the measurement thresholds.
- That a new brand can plan fame; fame is built by consistent use over time, which a CI can only prepare for.

## Why it matters for ORIVILO

Category differentiation is a measurable reason to choose a brand colour, separate from colour meaning ([[K-0038-color-meaning-is-learned-and-culture-specific]]). `brand-ci-method.md` §5 already audits the category's hues and plans three to five distinctive assets; this entry is the evidence behind that idea.
